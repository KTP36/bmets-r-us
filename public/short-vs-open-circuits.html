Short vs. Open Circuits

Both faults can leave a load off. Follow the current path to tell them apart.

Follow the current path

Remember: An open is a break in a path. A short is an unintended low-resistance connection. A closed switch is normal; it isn’t automatically a fault.

What would a meter tell you?

In a de-energized training circuit with the part isolated, OL across a wire that should conduct suggests an open. Near-zero resistance across two conductors that should be separate suggests a short. A continuity beep alone doesn’t prove a fault: intact wires and some components legitimately have low resistance.

BENCH CHALLENGE · 1 MINUTE

The lamp is dark. What will the meter read?

A 6 V DC supply is on. The lamp and its return wire are intact, but the wire from the positive terminal has a break. A digital multimeter is set to DC volts, with one probe on each side of the gap.

How does this differ from a short across the lamp?

A short adds a low-resistance path between the lamp terminals. The voltage across the lamp may drop close to zero, or protection may shut the supply down. A dark lamp alone cannot tell you which fault occurred.

Bench takeaway: A gap breaks the current path. A short adds an unintended path. Location matters: say exactly where the meter probes are and which mode the meter is in.
