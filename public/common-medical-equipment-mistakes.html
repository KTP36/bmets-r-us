13 Common Medical Equipment Mistakes to Notice First

Equipment problems are not always device failures. A loose connection, depleted battery, poorly placed sensor, wrong accessory, or patient movement can change what you see. Learn a safe first-look approach for nurses, students, and biomedical equipment learners.

Before calling it a failure:
  1. Look at the patient and situation.
  2. Check power and connections.
  3. Check the correct sensor or accessory.
  4. Follow policy and escalate concerns.
Safety first: Never delay patient care to troubleshoot equipment. Follow your facility's policies, the manufacturer's instructions, and your scope of practice. Remove unsafe equipment from service and notify the appropriate clinical or biomedical team.

Common mistakes and what to notice first

1

Device has no display or will not turn on

A dark display can look like a major failure, but start with the complete power path.

  • Loose or unplugged power cord
  • Power strip or outlet is off
  • Device switch is off
  • Battery is depleted or not seated
Notice first: Confirm power at the wall, intermediate connections, and device before assuming an internal fault.
2

Monitor shows “No Signal”

“No signal” often points to the connection between the patient, accessory, and monitor—not the monitor itself.

  • Patient cable is disconnected
  • Sensor is not attached correctly
  • Wrong input or source is selected
  • Accessory is damaged or incompatible
Notice first: Trace the signal path from the patient to the display.
3

SpO2 reading does not look right

Pulse oximetry can be affected by placement, motion, perfusion, ambient light, nail products, or an inappropriate sensor.

  • Poor sensor positioning
  • Patient movement
  • Low peripheral perfusion
  • Sensor or cable problem
Notice first: Compare the waveform or signal-quality indicator and the patient's condition before trusting a number in isolation.
4

EKG leads are misplaced or loose

Incorrect placement, dry electrodes, loose snaps, or reversed leads can create misleading tracings and artifact.

  • Electrodes are dry or expired
  • Skin preparation is inadequate
  • Lead wires are loose or reversed
  • Placement does not match the intended configuration
Notice first: Verify placement and connections using your facility's approved reference.
5

Blood pressure readings are inconsistent

Cuff size, position, movement, tubing, and patient factors all influence noninvasive blood pressure readings.

  • Cuff is too large or too small
  • Cuff is loose or over clothing
  • Arm is not supported appropriately
  • Tubing is kinked or leaking
Notice first: Check cuff size and placement, then repeat according to policy if the reading conflicts with the clinical picture.
6

IV pump keeps alarming

An alarm is information. Silencing it without identifying the cause can hide a continuing problem.

  • Closed clamp or kinked tubing
  • Downstream occlusion
  • Air-in-line detection
  • Door, cassette, or set is loaded incorrectly
Notice first: Read the alarm message, assess the patient and line, and follow the device instructions and facility policy.
7

Wrong cable, sensor, or accessory is connected

Connectors that look similar are not necessarily interchangeable. Compatibility and intended use matter.

  • Wrong patient cable generation
  • Incorrect sensor type or size
  • Adapter not approved for the device
  • Damaged pins or connector housing
Notice first: Match the accessory to the device labeling and approved inventory—never force a connector.
8

Equipment failure is assumed too quickly

Simple setup issues can imitate a failure and lead to unnecessary downtime or service calls.

  • Control or mode is set incorrectly
  • Required accessory is missing
  • User message has not been read
  • Device needs an approved restart procedure
Notice first: Capture the exact symptom or message and complete approved basic checks before escalation.
9

Battery status is ignored

A device may work while plugged in but shut down during transport because the battery cannot support the load.

  • Battery was not charged
  • Battery is aging or damaged
  • Charging indicator was misunderstood
  • Device was stored unplugged
Notice first: Check charge level and battery warnings before transport or mobile use.
10

Symbols and indicators are misread

Icons for alarms, muted audio, battery state, connectivity, sensor status, and service needs can be easy to overlook.

  • Alarm audio is paused or silenced
  • Battery is charging versus fully charged
  • Sensor-disconnected indicator is active
  • Network status is confused with device function
Notice first: Use the device's labeling and instructions rather than guessing what an unfamiliar symbol means.
11

The reading is not compared with the situation

A plausible-looking number can still be wrong. Equipment data should be interpreted with the patient assessment and other available information.

  • Value changes suddenly without a clinical change
  • Multiple devices disagree
  • Waveform quality is poor
  • Displayed value does not fit observed symptoms
Notice first: Assess the patient and validate unexpected results through the appropriate clinical process.
12

Movement artifact is overlooked

Motion, vibration, cable movement, and muscle activity can distort physiologic measurements.

  • EKG baseline becomes irregular
  • Pulse oximeter signal becomes unstable
  • Noninvasive blood pressure cycle fails
  • Respiration or other derived values jump unexpectedly
Notice first: Look for movement and signal-quality changes occurring at the same time.
13

The obvious checks are skipped

Good troubleshooting is a repeatable process, not a guess. Start with the patient and the simplest external causes before moving toward complex explanations.

1. AssessPatient, urgency, environment, and displayed message.
2. VerifyPower, connections, setup, sensor, and approved accessories.
3. DocumentExact symptom, alarm, conditions, and steps already taken.
4. EscalateFollow policy; remove unsafe equipment from service and notify the proper team.
Notice first: A consistent first-look process reduces assumptions and improves communication when escalation is needed.
