The EMT Lens

Ask four questions:
Where is the problem? → What system is involved? → What is that system supposed to do? → What does failure look like?
Lesson 2.1

Medical Terminology for EMS

Medical terminology gives responders a shared language. Breaking a word into parts often provides clues to its meaning, but EMT communication also depends on simple descriptive terms that identify location, symptoms, and changes in condition.

Brady-Slow
Tachy-Fast
Hypo-Below / deficient
Hyper-Above / excessive
-pneaBreathing
-cardiaHeart rate / heart condition
Example: Tachypnea describes abnormally rapid breathing. Bradycardia describes a slow heart rate. Recognizing the pieces makes unfamiliar terms easier to interpret.

Lesson 2.2

Anatomical Position & Directional Terms

Directional terms prevent ambiguity. In standard anatomical position, the patient is considered upright, facing forward, with arms at the sides and palms forward. Right and left refer to the patient's right and left.

AnteriorToward the front.
PosteriorToward the back.
SuperiorToward the head / above.
InferiorToward the feet / below.
MedialToward the midline.
LateralAway from the midline.
ProximalCloser to the trunk or point of origin.
DistalFarther from the trunk or point of origin.
EMS Translation: “Pain distal to the right elbow” communicates location more precisely than “pain down the arm.”
Anatomical-language lesson completed.
Lesson 2.3

Airway & Respiratory System

The respiratory system moves air and supports gas exchange. For an EMT, three concepts matter immediately: the airway must be open, ventilation must move air effectively, and oxygen must reach the bloodstream and tissues.

Upper AirwayNose, mouth, pharynx, and structures leading toward the larynx.
Lower AirwayTrachea, bronchi, bronchioles, and lungs.
VentilationMovement of air into and out of the lungs.
Gas ExchangeOxygen and carbon dioxide move between alveoli and blood.
Failure clues: Abnormal respiratory rate or effort, inability to speak normally, altered mental status, poor air movement, abnormal skin findings, or worsening fatigue can signal a serious breathing problem.
Respiratory-system lesson completed.
Lesson 2.4

Cardiovascular System & Perfusion

The cardiovascular system moves blood through the body. The heart provides the pump, blood vessels provide the pathway, and blood carries oxygen and other substances to tissues.

HeartPumps blood through pulmonary and systemic circulation.
ArteriesCarry blood away from the heart.
VeinsReturn blood toward the heart.
CapillariesSmall vessels where exchange with tissues occurs.
Perfusion: Think “delivery.” Adequate perfusion means tissues are receiving enough oxygenated blood to meet their needs.
Poor-perfusion clues: Weak pulses, cool or pale skin, altered mental status, delayed peripheral circulation, or other signs of circulatory compromise should raise concern in the clinical context.
Cardiovascular lesson completed.
Lesson 2.5

Nervous System

The nervous system controls awareness, movement, sensation, breathing drive, and many automatic functions. The brain and spinal cord form the central nervous system; nerves extending throughout the body connect the CNS with tissues and organs.

BrainCoordinates consciousness, thought, movement, sensation, and vital functions.
Spinal CordMajor communication pathway between brain and body.
Peripheral NervesCarry sensory and motor information.
Neurologic warning signs: New altered mental status, weakness, speech difficulty, seizure activity, abnormal responsiveness, or loss of normal movement/sensation can indicate serious neurologic dysfunction.
Nervous-system lesson completed.
Lesson 2.6

Musculoskeletal System

Bones provide structure and protection, joints permit movement, and muscles generate movement. In trauma, EMTs also think about what important structures lie beneath or beside an injured bone or joint.

SkullProtects the brain.
SpineSupports the body and protects the spinal cord.
ThoraxRibs and sternum help protect the heart and lungs.
PelvisSupports the trunk and protects major vessels and pelvic organs.
EMT connection: A musculoskeletal injury can be more than a “broken bone.” Think about bleeding, circulation, nerve function, underlying organs, and mechanism of injury.
Musculoskeletal lesson completed.
Lesson 2.7

Abdominal Organs & Major Body Systems

The abdomen contains organs from several systems. EMTs use location, history, mechanism, and patient findings to guide assessment rather than trying to make a definitive diagnosis in the field.

LiverLarge upper-right abdominal organ with metabolic, storage, and blood-related functions.
SpleenUpper-left abdominal organ associated with blood and immune functions.
Stomach & IntestinesDigest and absorb nutrients.
KidneysFilter blood, regulate fluid/electrolyte balance, and produce urine.
PancreasSupports digestion and blood-glucose regulation.
BladderStores urine before elimination.
Assessment principle: Know what structures are likely in a region, but treat the patient and findings—not an assumed diagnosis.
Abdominal-systems lesson completed.
Lesson 2.8

How Body Systems Fail

Emergency patients often present with a failure of function rather than a neat diagnostic label. Recognizing the pattern helps an EMT identify priorities.

Airway FailureAir cannot move freely through the airway.
Ventilatory FailureBreathing does not move enough air.
Oxygenation ProblemOxygen transfer or delivery is inadequate.
Perfusion FailureTissues do not receive adequate blood flow.
Neurologic FailureNormal brain or nervous-system function is impaired.
Do not wait for a diagnosis: If a patient cannot maintain an airway, breathe adequately, perfuse vital organs, or maintain normal neurologic function, that functional problem becomes the priority.
System-failure lesson completed.
