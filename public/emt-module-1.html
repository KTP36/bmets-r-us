Lesson 1.1

What EMTs Do

An EMT provides basic emergency medical care in the out-of-hospital environment. The job begins before any treatment: receive information, approach safely, assess the patient, identify immediate threats, provide care within training and local protocol, communicate findings, and help transport the patient appropriately.

AssessRecognize what is happening now.
PrioritizeAddress immediate threats first.
TreatProvide care within training and protocol.
ReassessWatch for improvement or deterioration.
CommunicateShare accurate, useful information.
TransportMove the patient toward definitive care.
EMT Mindset: The goal is not to name every diagnosis. The goal is to recognize threats, support the patient, and make safe decisions with the information available.

Lesson 1.2

The EMS System & Levels of Care

Emergency medical services are a coordinated system. Dispatch, first responders, EMRs, EMTs, advanced clinicians, fire/rescue, law enforcement, hospitals, specialty teams, and medical direction may all contribute to one patient's care.

Know Your Role: EMTs work as part of a larger system. The safest answer is often to request the right resource early rather than trying to solve every problem alone.

Examples of Additional Resources

  • Advanced life support when the patient's condition exceeds basic-level capability.
  • Fire/rescue for entrapment, access, lifting, fire, or technical hazards.
  • Law enforcement for violence, weapons, unsafe crowds, or scene control.
  • Hazardous-materials resources when a substance or atmosphere may be dangerous.
  • Additional ambulances or command resources for multiple patients.

Lesson 1.3

Roles, Responsibilities & Scope

Training gives an EMT a defined set of skills, but actual practice is also shaped by state rules, medical direction, employer policy, and local protocol. A safe EMT knows both what to do and when to stop and escalate.

Professional Rule: Do not perform a procedure simply because you have seen it done. Use only skills you are trained, authorized, equipped, and directed to perform.

Core Responsibilities

  • Protect yourself, your partner, the patient, and bystanders.
  • Work within current training and protocol.
  • Maintain patient privacy and dignity.
  • Communicate honestly and accurately.
  • Ask for help when a situation exceeds available resources.
  • Document what you observed, did, and communicated.

Lesson 1.4

Consent, Refusal & Patient Rights

Patients generally have the right to understand proposed care and participate in decisions when they have decision-making capacity. Consent, refusal, minors, altered mental status, and emergency situations can become complex, so EMTs must follow training, law, medical direction, and local protocol.

ExplainTell the patient what you are doing in clear language.
RespectProtect privacy, dignity, and reasonable preferences.
Assess CapacityDetermine whether the patient can understand and decide.
EscalateUse medical direction or policy when consent is unclear.
Important: Consent and refusal requirements can vary by jurisdiction and situation. Use your approved EMS education, local protocol, and medical direction.

Lesson 1.5

Standard Precautions & PPE

Responder protection begins before touching the patient. Consider the likely exposure and choose appropriate barriers. Gloves are common, but the situation may also require eye protection, masks, gowns, or other protective equipment.

Blood / Body FluidsUse appropriate barrier protection.
Splash RiskProtect eyes and face.
Respiratory ConcernUse appropriate respiratory protection per policy.
Unknown HazardDo not enter until the hazard is identified and controlled.
Before Patient Contact: Ask yourself, “What can hurt me here?” and “What protection or resource do I need before I go closer?”

Lesson 1.6

Scene Safety & Size-Up

Scene safety is not a single checkbox. Conditions can change. Traffic, fire, unstable structures, electricity, violence, animals, weather, hazardous materials, crowds, and environmental hazards may place responders at risk.

Before You Commit to the Scene

  • Look and listen before approaching.
  • Consider the mechanism of injury or nature of illness.
  • Determine the number of patients.
  • Identify hazards and escape routes.
  • Decide whether additional resources are needed.
  • Reassess safety throughout the call.
Scene-Safety Principle: An injured rescuer cannot help the original patient and may create another patient. Do not enter an uncontrolled dangerous scene simply because someone needs help.

Lesson 1.7

Communication & Documentation Foundations

Good EMS communication is concise, objective, timely, and useful. Whether speaking with a patient, calling for resources, giving a radio report, or handing off care, organize information around what the next person needs to know.

DispatchConfirm location, call type, updates, and requested resources.
PatientUse calm, understandable, respectful language.
Radio / HandoffShare key findings, trends, care, and response.
DocumentationRecord objective facts, observations, actions, times, and response.
Documentation Pearl: Write what you observed and what happened. Avoid unsupported assumptions or judgmental language.
Communication-and-documentation lesson completed.
Lesson 1.8

Professionalism & EMT Readiness

EMT readiness includes more than clinical knowledge. Reliability, teamwork, situational awareness, emotional control, physical readiness, respect, honesty, preparation, and a willingness to learn all influence patient care.

Before the Call

  • Know the equipment and where it is stored.
  • Confirm essential supplies are ready.
  • Know local procedures for requesting help.
  • Maintain situational awareness during every response.
  • Communicate concerns early.
  • Debrief and learn from difficult calls within appropriate support systems.
What good EMTs keep doing: Learn, practice, ask questions, review mistakes, and keep safety at the center of every call.
