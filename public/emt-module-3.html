Module 3: Airway, Respiration & Ventilation

Airway questions become challenging when several answers sound reasonable. This module focuses on the decision that matters most: recognize whether the patient is protecting the airway, moving enough air, oxygenating adequately, or beginning to fail.

Learning Objectives

  • Separate airway patency, ventilation, oxygenation, and respiration as related concepts.
  • Recognize adequate versus inadequate breathing from the entire patient presentation.
  • Identify common signs of airway obstruction and respiratory compromise.
  • Understand suction, basic airway adjuncts, oxygen delivery, and BVM ventilation at the EMT level.
  • Differentiate respiratory distress from worsening respiratory failure.
  • Prioritize airway and breathing interventions in scenario-based questions.

The Challenge

Do not choose an intervention from one number alone. Look at mental status, respiratory effort, chest rise, air movement, skin findings, ability to speak, and whether the patient is tiring.

Think in order: Is the airway open? → Is the patient moving enough air? → Is oxygenation adequate? → Are they improving or failing?
Lesson 3.1

Airway vs. Ventilation vs. Oxygenation

These concepts overlap, but they are not interchangeable. A patient can have an open airway and still ventilate poorly. A patient can visibly breathe yet still have inadequate oxygenation.

AirwayThe pathway that allows air to move between the environment and lungs.
VentilationThe mechanical movement of air into and out of the lungs.
OxygenationGetting oxygen into the blood so it can be delivered to tissues.
RespirationGas exchange processes involving oxygen and carbon dioxide.
Exam clue: “The patient is breathing” does not prove ventilation is adequate. Ask whether there is enough rate, depth, chest rise, and air movement to sustain the patient.
Core concepts completed.
Lesson 3.2

Assessing the Airway

An airway assessment starts with observation and listening. A patient who speaks clearly usually demonstrates some airway patency, but airway status can deteriorate rapidly.

Potentially PatentClear speech, effective airflow, no obvious obstruction.
ThreatenedSwelling, trauma, secretions, vomit, blood, reduced consciousness, or progressive difficulty.
ObstructedMarkedly reduced or absent airflow with signs of blockage.
Important: Do not become distracted by oxygen equipment when the airway itself is not open. Air must have a pathway before oxygen delivery can be effective.
Airway assessment completed.
Lesson 3.3

Airway Positioning, Suction & Basic Adjuncts

Basic airway care may include positioning, clearing visible material, suction, and use of an appropriate airway adjunct according to training and protocol.

PositionUse an airway-opening approach appropriate to the patient's condition and suspected injury.
SuctionRemove blood, vomit, or secretions that interfere with airway patency.
OPAA basic adjunct generally considered for an unresponsive patient without an intact gag reflex, per training/protocol.
NPAA nasal airway adjunct used in appropriate patients when not contraindicated, per training/protocol.
Priority clue: If fluid is preventing effective ventilation, simply placing an adjunct without addressing the contamination does not solve the problem.
Basic airway interventions completed.
Lesson 3.4

Is Breathing Adequate?

Respiratory rate matters, but rate alone is not enough. Evaluate depth, effort, rhythm, chest rise, breath sounds/air movement, mental status, skin findings, and the patient's ability to speak.

More ReassuringMore Concerning
Appropriate rate and depth for the patientVery slow, very fast, irregular, or deteriorating pattern
Good chest rise and air movementShallow respirations or poor chest rise
Normal interaction and speechAltered mental status or inability to speak normally
Work of breathing is sustainableSevere effort, fatigue, or decreasing effort despite continued distress
Challenging clue: A respiratory rate can move closer to “normal” because the patient is improving—or because the patient is tiring and failing. The rest of the assessment tells you which.
Breathing assessment completed.
Lesson 3.5

Oxygen Delivery Devices

Supplemental oxygen is selected based on patient need, clinical presentation, equipment, training, and local protocol. The device matters less than understanding the problem you are trying to correct.

Nasal CannulaSupplemental oxygen for appropriate spontaneously breathing patients.
Nonrebreather MaskHigher-concentration supplemental oxygen for appropriate patients with adequate spontaneous ventilation.
BVM + OxygenUsed to assist or provide ventilation when spontaneous ventilation is inadequate or absent.
Decision point: Oxygen and ventilation are not the same treatment. A patient who is not moving enough air may need ventilatory assistance, not simply a higher oxygen concentration.
Oxygen-delivery lesson completed.
Lesson 3.6

Bag-Valve-Mask Ventilation

BVM ventilation is a critical EMT skill used when a patient is not ventilating adequately. Effective BVM use depends on an open airway, appropriate positioning, a good mask seal, visible chest rise, and reassessment.

Quality matters: Squeezing a bag does not guarantee effective ventilation. Watch the patient. Is the chest rising appropriately? Is air entering? Is the clinical condition improving?

Common Reasons Ventilation May Be Ineffective

  • Poor mask seal.
  • Airway not adequately opened or maintained.
  • Airway obstruction or contamination.
  • Technique that does not produce appropriate chest rise.
  • Failure to reassess the patient's response.
BVM lesson completed.
Lesson 3.7

Respiratory Distress vs. Respiratory Failure

Respiratory distress means the patient is working harder to maintain breathing. Respiratory failure develops when the patient can no longer maintain adequate ventilation or oxygenation.

Respiratory DistressWorsening Toward Failure
Increased effortFatigue or decreasing respiratory effort
Tachypnea may be presentRate may become irregular, slow, or otherwise inadequate
Patient may remain alertAltered or decreasing mental status
Air movement may still be adequatePoor air movement / shallow breathing
Do not be fooled by a quieter patient: Someone who was fighting to breathe and then becomes drowsy with weaker effort may be getting worse, not better.
Distress-vs-failure lesson completed.
Lesson 3.8

Putting the Airway Decision Together

EMT airway decisions are based on priorities rather than isolated facts. When several abnormalities exist, identify the one that most immediately threatens effective breathing.

1. Open?Can air move through the airway?
2. Clear?Are secretions, blood, vomit, swelling, or an object interfering?
3. Adequate?Is the patient moving enough air?
4. Oxygenating?Are clinical findings consistent with adequate oxygenation?
5. Trending?Is the patient improving, stable, tiring, or deteriorating?
Exam strategy: If one answer provides oxygen and another actually corrects inadequate ventilation, determine whether the patient is still ventilating effectively before choosing.
Airway decision framework completed.

Module 3 Final Quiz

This one is intentionally a little harder. Most questions require you to interpret findings rather than simply recall a definition. Score 80% or higher to complete Module 3.

1. An adult is unresponsive with slow, shallow respirations and poor chest rise. Which problem is the immediate priority?
Slow shallow breathing with poor chest rise indicates inadequate ventilation; support airway and breathing per training/protocol.
2. Which statement best distinguishes oxygenation from ventilation?
Ventilation is air movement; oxygenation refers to oxygen entering blood and being available for delivery.
3. A patient was breathing 34/min with severe effort. Ten minutes later the rate is 18/min, but the patient is now drowsy with shallow respirations and poor air movement. Best interpretation?
A more normal-looking rate can be misleading when effort, depth, air movement, and mental status are worsening.
4. An unresponsive patient has vomit in the mouth and ineffective respirations. Which issue should be addressed before expecting BVM ventilation to work effectively?
Airway contamination can prevent effective ventilation; airway clearance is a priority.
5. Which finding most strongly suggests that a patient in respiratory distress is beginning to fail?
Decreasing consciousness and weakening ventilation are ominous signs of respiratory failure.
6. A spontaneously breathing patient has severe dyspnea but good chest rise and adequate air movement. Which concept is most important before deciding on a device?
The distinction between adequate and inadequate spontaneous ventilation drives whether oxygen support or assisted ventilation is needed.
7. During BVM ventilation, the chest is not rising. What is the best interpretation?
BVM effectiveness must be judged by the patient, including appropriate chest rise and air movement.
8. Which patient most clearly has an airway problem rather than an isolated oxygenation problem?
Markedly restricted airflow after choking indicates a direct airway threat.
9. Why can respiratory rate alone be misleading?
Breathing adequacy is determined from the whole clinical picture, not one number.
10. Which statement about an OPA is most appropriate at the EMT-learning level?
OPA use depends on patient responsiveness/gag reflex and local training/protocol.
11. A patient is breathing 8/min with shallow chest rise and becoming confused. Which choice best addresses the underlying immediate problem?
Slow shallow respirations plus altered mental status indicate inadequate ventilation that requires immediate support.
12. Which trend is most reassuring during reassessment?
Improved mentation, effort, and air movement together suggest a meaningful improvement.
13. A patient has an open airway but is taking occasional gasping breaths with little chest rise. Which statement is best?
The airway can be open while ventilation remains critically inadequate.
14. What is the strongest reason to reassess after an airway or breathing intervention?
Interventions must be evaluated by their effect on the patient.
15. A patient with severe respiratory distress is alert but tiring. Over several minutes, speech decreases from short sentences to single words and air movement becomes weaker. What matters most?
A deteriorating trend in speech, fatigue, and air movement is more important than a single isolated measurement.
