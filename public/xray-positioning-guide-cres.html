Foundation

1. Positioning Controls the Projection

The X-ray image is a two-dimensional projection of three-dimensional anatomy. The relationship among focal spot, anatomy, central ray, and detector determines what overlaps, what separates, and how large or distorted structures appear.

Source

The focal spot produces a diverging beam. Centering and angulation shape the projection.

Object

Patient rotation and the location of anatomy affect superimposition, size, and shape.

Detector

Distance and alignment affect recorded detail, field coverage, distortion, and magnification.

Key principle: Positioning changes the appearance of anatomy—not the anatomy itself.
Projection Logic

Projection names describe the direction the central ray travels through the patient.

Remember: Two views at roughly 90° help localize a finding in three-dimensional space.
High-Yield Physics

3. SID, SOD, OID, and Magnification

SID

Source-to-image distance

Focal spot to detector.

SOD

Source-to-object distance

Focal spot to anatomy.

OID

Object-to-image distance

Anatomy to detector.

Magnification Factor = SID ÷ SOD
ChangeMagnificationDetailWhy
Increase OIDIncreasesUsually decreasesThe shadow expands before reaching the detector
Decrease OIDDecreasesUsually improvesThe object is closer to the detector
Increase SIDDecreasesUsually improvesBeam divergence has less geometric effect
Decrease SIDIncreasesUsually decreasesBeam divergence has more geometric effect

Worked example

A structure is imaged at 100 cm SID and 80 cm SOD. MF = 100 ÷ 80 = 1.25. The image is 1.25 times actual size, or 25% magnified.

Common Confusion

4. Size Error vs Shape Error

Magnification

Changes apparent size. Look first at SID and OID.

Shape distortion

Changes proportions. Look at patient rotation, anatomy-detector alignment, and central-ray angle.

Foreshortening

The image appears shorter than the object because object, detector, and ray alignment is incorrect.

Elongation

The image appears longer than the object, often from incorrect central-ray angulation.

Fast distinction: Size error suggests distance. Shape error suggests alignment.
Clinical Application

5. Why Portable Images Look Different

Common conditions

  • AP instead of PA projection
  • Supine or semi-erect patient
  • Greater OID or inconsistent SID
  • Harder rotation control
  • More scatter when collimation or grid alignment is suboptimal

Possible consequences

  • Apparent cardiac enlargement
  • Reduced contrast
  • Rotation and asymmetry
  • Motion blur
  • Subtle redistribution of air and fluid
Gravity matters: Upright positioning makes air rise and fluid settle. Supine positioning redistributes both, which can make some findings less obvious.

System Interaction

8. Grid, Collimation, and AEC Problems

Grid cutoff

Off-level, off-center, off-focus, or upside-down focused grids create different exposure-loss patterns.

Field mismatch

A centered light field can still clip anatomy when the light and radiation fields are not congruent.

AEC placement

Poor anatomy placement over the selected chamber can end exposure too early or too late.

PatternLikely categoryControlled check
Uniform density lossOff-focus/off-level grid or techniqueConfirm SID, focal range, orientation, and perpendicular alignment

Technician Workflow

9. Separate Technique From Equipment Failure

Do not replace components from one rejected image. Reproduce the condition under controlled geometry and change one variable at a time.

1. DefineRecord projection, SID, OID, angulation, receptor, grid, AEC chamber, technique, and exact symptom.
2. ReproduceUse a stable phantom and the same setup. A nonreproducible problem may be workflow-dependent.
3. IsolateUse manual technique if AEC is suspected; compare approved grids, detectors, or rooms when appropriate.
4. MeasureVerify SID indication, centering, beam alignment, collimator congruence, calibration status, and motion.
5. DocumentRecord test conditions and compare results with manufacturer and facility QC requirements.
Safety: Follow the service manual, radiation-safety program, and facility procedures. Never expose a patient merely to reproduce a service complaint.
CRES Troubleshooting

6. Read the Clue, Then Trace the Cause

EnlargementCheck projection, OID, SID, and object-to-detector position.
AsymmetryCheck patient rotation, centering, and detector alignment.
Motion blurCheck motion, exposure time, immobilization, and mechanical stability.
Shape distortionCheck central-ray angle and source-object-detector alignment.
Low contrastCheck scatter, field size, thickness, grid alignment, and technique.
Clipped anatomyCheck centering, collimation, detector orientation, and movement.
Order of operations: Confirm the complaint → inspect positioning and acquisition → compare with known-good technique → look for repeat patterns → investigate equipment.
Applied Practice

7. CRES Scenarios

Portable AP chest: the heart looks larger than on an earlier PA image. Why?

AP positioning increases heart-to-detector distance, producing greater magnification. Projection should be considered before assuming true enlargement.

One hemithorax appears wider and the clavicles are asymmetric. What should you check?

Patient rotation. Reposition and verify symmetry before investigating hardware.

An object appears larger and less sharp after it is raised from the detector. What changed?

OID increased, causing more magnification and geometric unsharpness.

Repeated cutoff occurs when the tube is angled across grid strips. What is likely?

Grid cutoff from misalignment. Check tube-grid centering, angulation, grid orientation, focal range, and SID.

Knowledge Check

8. Test the Relationships

1. What happens when OID increases?

Magnification increases and recorded detail usually decreases.

2. Why does PA chest reduce heart magnification?

The heart is closer to the detector, reducing OID.

3. Which view adds depth to a frontal image?

A lateral view.

4. What commonly creates image asymmetry?

Patient rotation.

5. What happens to magnification when SID increases?

It generally decreases when the other geometric relationships are controlled.

6. An image is elongated. Check exposure or alignment first?

Alignment. Elongation is shape distortion.

Quick Review

Distance controls size

More OID means more magnification. More SID generally means less.

Alignment controls shape

Rotation and incorrect ray alignment produce predictable distortion.

Position controls appearance

AP, PA, lateral, oblique, upright, and supine views change projection.
