How to read an ECG rhythm strip

EKG and ECG both mean electrocardiogram: a recording of the heart’s electrical activity. Use the same sequence for each practice strip.

  1. Confirm the recording. Check the time scale, calibration, lead, and signal quality. A distorted trace may be artifact.
  2. Estimate the rate. For a regular rhythm at 25 mm/s, use 300 divided by large boxes between R waves, or 1500 divided by small boxes. For a six-second strip, counting QRS complexes and multiplying by ten gives an approximate rate, especially useful when irregular.
  3. Check regularity. Compare several R–R intervals, not just two beats.
  4. Find atrial activity. Look for consistent P waves and their relationship to the QRS complexes.
  5. Measure conduction. Inspect the PR interval and QRS duration. An adult QRS of 0.12 seconds or more is wide.
  6. Combine the clues. Choose a rhythm supported by the complete pattern, not a single feature.

In sinus rhythm, the impulse starts in the SA node and travels through the atria, AV node, His–Purkinje system, and ventricles. Other rhythms can start elsewhere. The P wave reflects atrial depolarization, the QRS ventricular depolarization, and the T wave ventricular repolarization.

Common ECG rhythms at a glance

RhythmTypical patternImportant qualification
Normal sinus rhythmRegular, consistent P before each QRS; resting adult rate commonly 60–100/min.Check atrial and ventricular relationships, not rate alone.
Sinus bradycardiaSinus pattern with an adult rate below 60/min.Its significance depends on symptoms and context.
Sinus tachycardiaSinus pattern with an adult rate above 100/min.A fast rate alone does not identify the rhythm or its cause.
Atrial fibrillationIrregularly irregular ventricular response without consistent discrete P waves.Some other rhythms are also irregular; inspect atrial activity.
Atrial flutterOrganized flutter activity, often sawtooth in suitable leads.The ventricular response may be regular or variable.
Premature ventricular beatAn early, typically wide ventricular complex.Assess the surrounding rhythm and full tracing.
Ventricular tachycardiaA rapid rhythm of ventricular origin, typically with wide complexes.A wide-complex tachycardia can have other causes.
Ventricular fibrillationDisorganized activity without identifiable organized QRS complexes.Signal quality and the patient’s condition matter.
AsystoleAbsence of discernible ventricular electrical activity.A near-flat trace also requires checking leads, connections, and equipment.

Beyond the pattern: why context matters

Wide and fast is a description, not a complete diagnosis

A regular wide-complex rhythm at 180/min should raise concern for ventricular tachycardia, but rate and width alone do not prove VT. Supraventricular rhythms with abnormal conduction or pre-excitation can also be wide. Clinical assessment and additional ECG evidence guide qualified interpretation.

A monitor trace is not the whole patient

Motion, muscle activity, poor electrode contact, and electrical interference can distort a tracing. In clinical work, assess the patient and recording quality and follow approved training and local protocols. Do not make treatment or return-to-service decisions from these practice examples.

A rhythm strip is not a complete 12-lead interpretation

These exercises teach basic rhythm recognition. They do not establish ischemia, infarction, electrolyte abnormalities, or a treatment plan from a single visual clue. Use the linked learning resources for further study and qualified instruction for clinical decisions.
